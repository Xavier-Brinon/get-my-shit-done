* Introduction

This document is the GMSD single source of truth for the Diataxis
documentation framework. All downstream phases — classification (Phase
2), gap filling (Phase 3), and internal doc reorganisation (Phase 4) —
draw their quadrant definitions, writing rules, and anti-patterns from
this reference.

Diataxis organises documentation into four distinct quadrants defined
by two axes: *practical vs theoretical* and *studying vs working*.

#+CAPTION: Diataxis 2x2 quadrant map
#+NAME: tab:diataxis-quadrants
| Knowledge type       | Useful when studying | Useful when working |
|----------------------+----------------------+---------------------|
| *Practical*          | Tutorial             | How-to              |
| *Theoretical*        | Explanation          | Reference           |

Each quadrant serves a different reader need. Mixing quadrants in a
single document almost always reduces usefulness for every audience.

* Tutorial
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: tutorial
:END:

** Definition

A tutorial is *learning-oriented*. It takes the reader through a
series of steps to complete a meaningful project. The reader learns by
doing — their primary outcome is new capability and confidence, not a
specific deliverable.

The teacher (author) is in control. The learner follows the teacher's
chosen path to reach a guaranteed outcome. The outcome matters less
than the journey.

** Writing rules

- *Start with a concrete action* — the learner should do something
  real in the first few minutes. Waiting builds anxiety, not
  confidence.
- *Let the learner do things* — hands-on experience builds capability
  faster than reading. Every instruction should prompt an action.
- *Provide a working result at each step* — the learner needs to see
  that what they did worked before moving on. Visible progress
  sustains motivation.
- *Explain only what is necessary to proceed* — save deeper
  explanation for Explanation documents. Interrupting the flow with
  theory confuses learners about what matters right now.
- *Repeat key actions where appropriate* — repetition reinforces
  muscle memory. Doing something twice is not redundant in a tutorial.
- *Set realistic expectations up front* — state what the learner will
  build and what prior knowledge is assumed. Surprises mid-tutorial
  cause abandonment.

** Anti-patterns

*** Misclassification: tutorial mistaken for a how-to guide

*Warning:* A document that starts with "how to learn X" but assumes
the reader already has a specific goal in mind is not a tutorial — it
is a how-to guide wearing a tutorial's label.

*Test:* Does the reader need to know what they want before they start?
If yes, it is a how-to. A tutorial reader does not yet know what they
want — they trust the teacher to lead them.

*Fix:* If the reader needs a specific goal to use the document, recast
it as a how-to guide. Remove the assumed context and add onboarding
scaffolding to write it as a true tutorial.

*** Writing pitfall: overloading with explanation

*Warning:* A tutorial that pauses to discuss why something works,
explore trade-offs, or define terms at length stops being a tutorial
and becomes a lecture. Learners disengage when theory interrupts
action.

*Fix:* Move conceptual explanation into a separate Explanation
document and link to it with "to understand why this works, see
[Explanation doc]". Keep the tutorial moving.

** Example

/Generic:/ "Bake your first loaf of bread" — the reader follows every
step in order (mix, knead, prove, bake) and ends with a real loaf.
They learn bread-baking by making bread, not by reading about it.

/GMSD mapping:/ A tutorial for GMSD would walk a first-time user
through running =/gmsd:start= on a brand-new project from zero to
first committed plan. The outcome (a committed plan) is real but
secondary — the primary outcome is the user's confidence in the
workflow.

* How-to
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: how-to
:END:

** Definition

A how-to guide is *task-oriented*. It takes a reader who already has a
goal through the steps needed to achieve it. The reader has a specific
problem or intention; the guide shows them how to accomplish it.

Unlike a tutorial, the reader is in control. The author assumes
competence and does not explain why each step works — the reader
trusts the sequence and wants to reach their goal efficiently.

** Writing rules

- *State the goal immediately* — the reader needs to confirm within
  the first sentence that this guide solves their problem. Do not
  bury the goal.
- *Assume prior competence* — skip onboarding. The reader does not
  need to know what a database is; they need to know how to add an
  index.
- *Give steps, not prose* — use numbered lists or clear imperative
  sentences. Narrative slows the reader down.
- *Keep steps minimal* — include only what is needed to achieve the
  goal. Every extra word increases cognitive load.
- *Note edge cases where they matter* — a brief "if X, do Y instead"
  is acceptable. A full explanation of why X exists belongs in an
  Explanation document.
- *Test every step* — ensure the guide works end-to-end before
  publishing. A broken step wastes the reader's time on a problem
  they did not come to solve.

** Anti-patterns

*** Misclassification: how-to mistaken for a tutorial

*Warning:* A guide that explains every concept as if the reader has
never encountered the topic before is not a how-to — it is a tutorial
in disguise. Adding background, motivation, and rationale turns a
focused guide into a course.

*Test:* Does the reader need to finish reading this to gain a new
ability, or do they just need to complete a task? Task completion
= how-to. Ability gain = tutorial.

*Fix:* Strip the background material into a separate Tutorial or
Explanation document. The how-to starts at "Step 1."

*** Writing pitfall: explaining concepts instead of giving steps

*Warning:* "Before you run this command, it is important to understand
that Docker containers are isolated environments that..." — the reader
already knows this, or they wouldn't be reading a how-to guide.

*Fix:* Replace explanatory paragraphs with action steps. If the
rationale is genuinely necessary for safe use, add it as a one-line
note, not a paragraph.

** Example

/Generic:/ "How to cook rice for 100 people" — the reader knows how
to cook rice; they need to scale it. The guide covers quantities,
equipment, and timing at scale. No explanation of what rice is or why
it absorbs water.

/GMSD mapping:/ "How to add a new phase to an existing project" — the
reader knows GMSD and wants to extend their project. The guide covers
the =/gmsd:add-phase= command, naming conventions, and linking the new
phase to existing ones.

* Reference
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: reference
:END:

** Definition

Reference material is *information-oriented*. It provides accurate,
complete, and austere technical descriptions of the system — its
components, interfaces, options, and behaviours. Reference is not
read from start to finish; it is consulted.

The standard is consistency and completeness. Every parameter,
return value, error code, or configuration option must be documented.
Omitting something is a defect.

** Writing rules

- *Describe, do not instruct* — reference describes what exists. It
  does not tell the reader to do things. "Returns a string" not "you
  should use this to get a string."
- *Be consistent* — every entry at the same level must have the same
  structure. Readers scan reference material; inconsistency breaks
  scanning.
- *Be complete* — document every option, even obscure ones. A reader
  who finds a gap cannot trust the reference.
- *Be accurate above all else* — an incorrect reference is worse than
  no reference. Verify against the implementation, not your memory.
- *Link to related how-to guides* — reference answers "what does this
  do?" and links to "how do I use this?" Mixing them pollutes both.
- *Use a machine-like, impersonal tone* — reference is not
  conversational. Avoid "you", hedging, and friendly asides.

** Anti-patterns

*** Misclassification: reference mistaken for explanation

*Warning:* A reference page that adds a "Background" or "Why this
exists" section has drifted into Explanation territory. Readers
consulting reference know the system exists — they need to look up
details, not understand history.

*Fix:* Move motivational or conceptual content into an Explanation
document. Link back from the reference: "For rationale, see
[Explanation doc]."

*** Writing pitfall: adding instructional steps

*Warning:* "To use this function, first import it, then call it with
your parameters..." — this is a how-to guide embedded in reference.
Reference describes the function's signature and behaviour; it does
not walk the reader through using it.

*Fix:* Replace procedural steps with declarative descriptions. Add a
link to a how-to guide for usage examples.

** Example

/Generic:/ A dictionary entry or an API specification — complete,
accurate, consulted not read. Every entry follows the same format.

/GMSD mapping:/ The =/gmsd:start= command reference: parameter names,
types, default values, environment variables, output format, and error
codes. No narrative, no tutorial steps.

* Explanation
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: explanation
:END:

** Definition

Explanation is *understanding-oriented*. It discusses a topic to
broaden the reader's understanding of it. The reader is not trying to
get something done — they want to understand why things are the way
they are, how concepts relate, or what trade-offs were made.

Explanation is not called when the reader has a specific goal. It is
read out of curiosity or to build mental models before engaging with
tutorials and how-to guides.

** Writing rules

- *Discuss, do not direct* — explanation illuminates; it does not
  instruct. Avoid imperative sentences. "The cache exists because..."
  not "add a cache."
- *Provide context and background* — explain history, motivation,
  alternative approaches considered, and trade-offs. This is where
  rationale lives.
- *Connect concepts to each other* — good explanation shows how ideas
  relate. A reader should finish the document with a richer mental
  model, not just a fact.
- *Accept that the reader may not act* — understanding is the goal.
  If the reader reaches the end without doing anything, the document
  succeeded.
- *Link to tutorials and how-to guides* — explanation is the place to
  link outward. "To put this into practice, see [Tutorial]."
- *Avoid jargon without definition* — explanation readers may not yet
  have the vocabulary. Define terms when they first appear.

** Anti-patterns

*** Misclassification: explanation mistaken for reference

*Warning:* An explanation document that lists every configuration
option, function signature, or parameter has become a reference page.
Reference catalogues; explanation discusses.

*Test:* Is the reader looking things up, or building understanding?
Look-up = reference. Understanding = explanation.

*Fix:* Move the catalogue into a Reference document. Keep in the
Explanation document only what illustrates the concept, not what
enumerates the system.

*** Writing pitfall: drifting into instruction

*Warning:* "To understand caching, first run this command..." —
explanation has become a tutorial. Explanation discusses; it does not
lead the reader through steps.

*Fix:* Remove imperative actions. If practice is valuable, link to a
tutorial or how-to guide: "To see this in action, try [Tutorial]."

** Example

/Generic:/ "About database normalisation" — the reader wants to
understand why relational databases are structured the way they are,
what normal forms exist, and what trade-offs the designer makes. They
are not creating a database right now.

/GMSD mapping:/ "Why GMSD uses the Diataxis framework" — discusses the
problem with undifferentiated documentation, how Diataxis solves it,
and what trade-offs were considered. A new contributor reads this
before touching any documentation.

* Tutorial vs How-to Disambiguation
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: tutorial-vs-howto
:END:

These two quadrants are the most frequently confused. Both are
practical and involve steps. The difference is the *reader's starting
point* and *who controls the path*.

#+CAPTION: Tutorial vs How-to contrast
#+NAME: tab:tutorial-vs-howto
| Dimension          | Tutorial                          | How-to                            |
|--------------------+-----------------------------------+-----------------------------------|
| Reader's goal      | None yet — trusts the teacher     | Specific — reader knows what they want |
| Reader's competence | None assumed — beginner           | Competence assumed                |
| Who controls path  | Author (teacher) controls         | Reader controls (author just guides) |
| Primary outcome    | New ability or confidence         | Task accomplished                 |
| Explanation allowed | Minimal — keep moving            | None — just the steps             |

*The key test:* "Does the reader know what they want to achieve?"

- *Yes* → write a how-to guide
- *No* → write a tutorial

A tutorial reader says "teach me how to do X." A how-to reader says
"I need to do X right now, show me how."

If readers arrive with a mix of prior knowledge, write both: a
tutorial for newcomers, a how-to for returning users solving a
specific problem.

Use this decision tree to classify any document or determine which
quadrant a new document should belong to.

flowchart TD
    A[What is the reader's need?] --> B{Practical or theoretical?}
    B -->|Practical — wants to do something| C{Studying or working?}
    B -->|Theoretical — wants to understand something| D{Studying or working?}
    C -->|Studying — learning a new skill| E[Tutorial\nLearning-oriented\nReader follows teacher's path]
    C -->|Working — has a specific goal| F[How-to\nTask-oriented\nReader achieves their goal]
    D -->|Studying — building mental models| G[Explanation\nUnderstanding-oriented\nReader broadens comprehension]
    D -->|Working — needs accurate details| H[Reference\nInformation-oriented\nReader looks up specifics]
#+end_src

If the flowchart is unavailable, use this text equivalent:

1. *Is the reader trying to do something or understand something?*
   - Do something → go to 2
   - Understand something → go to 3
2. *Is the reader learning a new skill or achieving a specific goal?*
   - Learning → *Tutorial*
   - Achieving a goal → *How-to*
3. *Is the reader building a mental model or looking up specifics?*
   - Building a mental model → *Explanation*
   - Looking up specifics → *Reference*

*Confirming your classification:*

- *Tutorial:* "After reading this, the reader will be able to [new
  skill]." Reader could not do this before.
- *How-to:* "After following these steps, the reader will have
  [accomplished goal]." Reader knew what they wanted.
- *Reference:* "The reader will find the exact [parameter/value/type]
  they were looking for." Reader was consulting, not learning.
- *Explanation:* "The reader will understand why [concept] works the
  way it does." Reader was curious, not goal-driven.
